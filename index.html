1 · Every attempt becomes 31 timings

The passphrase is the CMU benchmark’s .tie5Roanl plus Return: 11 keys. For each key KeyPrint records when it went down and up (browser event timestamps), then computes three kinds of interval, in the same column order as the CMU dataset:

11 hold times (H) + 10 press-to-press (DD) + 10 release-to-press (UD) = 31 features, in seconds. Keyup is matched by physical key, so letting go of Shift before R is fine. A typo, Backspace or leaving the field throws the attempt away; stray keys before “.” and Ctrl/Alt/Meta chords are ignored.

2 · Three detectors vote

  • Scaled Manhattan. Sum over features of |x − mean| divided by the mean absolute deviation. Simple and the best performer in Killourhy & Maxion’s comparison.
  • Mahalanobis. Distance to the mean that accounts for features moving together (a slow typist is slow everywhere).
  • Nearest neighbor. Mahalanobis distance to the closest single enrollment sample, which tolerates a profile with two styles.

Each detector accepts if its score is under its own threshold. The overall verdict is a 2-of-3 majority.

3 · Shrinkage covariance

With 10 samples and 31 features the sample covariance has rank 9 at most, so it cannot be inverted. KeyPrint shrinks the off-diagonal entries toward zero by a factor λ chosen from the data (Schäfer–Strimmer, clipped to at least 0.05), keeps the variances (floored at 1 ms SD), and inverts with a Cholesky factorization. On the full benchmark this also edges the paper’s plain-covariance Mahalanobis numbers.

4 · Thresholds from leave-one-out

To pick a threshold without asking you to type even more, KeyPrint trains on 9 of your 10 samples and scores the 10th, ten times. The threshold is the (1 − target FRR) quantile of those 10 out-of-sample scores, linearly interpolated: the 95th percentile for the default 5% target. The slider moves it live.

5 · Why 10 reps gives about twice the error

Mean EER on the CMU data with the same test set and only the training size changed:

A 95th percentile of 10 scores is noisy and biased low, and 10 reps typed in one sitting miss the drift between sessions. The false-reject rate only approaches its 5% target from about 100 reps.

6 · On a phone: keypad taps, 31 + 22 features

Soft keyboards report unreliable key timings (many send keydown and keyup together when the finger lifts, and autocorrect gets in the way), so on a touch screen KeyPrint shows its own keypad and never opens the system keyboard. Each key records pointerdown and pointerup with the event timestamp: hold = down → up, DD and UD exactly as on a keyboard, so the same 31 timing features. Overlapping taps (a second thumb landing before the first lifts) are allowed and give a negative UD, just like rolling keys on a keyboard.

On top of that, every tap records where it landed relative to the key centre, in key widths and heights (−0.5 … +0.5): an x and y offset for each of the 11 keys, 22 more features. The same three detectors run on the 53-number vector unchanged. Phone profiles are stored separately from keyboard profiles and are never scored against each other.

Whether tap position actually helps is an open question here: the CMU benchmark is keyboard-only. bench-mobile.js compares per-user EER with timing only against timing + position on 20-rep phone sessions, so far only on synthetic volunteers.

7 · Limits and privacy

  • Rhythm drifts across days, keyboards and moods; a profile from one sitting will reject you more often later.
  • This is a demo of a research technique, not production authentication. Use it alongside a password, never instead of one.
  • Desktop and phone profiles are kept apart and never compared: a keyboard rhythm and a thumb rhythm are different signals.
  • All timings and profiles stay in your browser (localStorage). Export and import are files you control.
