Detectors

When you verify, three detectors each compare your new try with your 10 enrollment tries and answer yes or no. Here is how each one thinks, worked through with a tiny example.

The example

The real page measures 31 timings per try. To keep the sums small, this example uses only two: how long you hold t, and the gap from letting go of t to pressing i. Here are your 10 enrollment tries, in milliseconds:

Try12345678910Average
Hold t10011288106941049610892100100
Gap t→i200218184208192206196214186196200

Look at try 2 and try 3. On a slow try you hold t longer and wait longer before i. On a fast try, both are shorter. Keep that in mind for detector 2.

  1. Scaled Manhattan: how many wobbles away?

    The idea. Your timings are never exactly the same twice. They wobble a little around your average. This detector takes each timing of the new try, sees how far it is from your average, and counts that distance in “your usual wobble”. Then it adds up the counts. A small total means the try looks like you.

    Your usual wobble is the average distance of your 10 tries from their average: 6 ms for the hold and 9.2 ms for the gap.

    New try A: hold 110, gap 220

    hold: (110 − 100) ÷ 6 = 1.67
    gap: (220 − 200) ÷ 9.2 = 2.17
    total = 3.84

    The line for this detector is 4.73. 3.84 is under it, so: YES

    New try D: hold 130, gap 260 (someone much slower)

    hold: (130 − 100) ÷ 6 = 5.00
    gap: (260 − 200) ÷ 9.2 = 6.52
    total = 11.52

    11.52 is over 4.73, so: NO

    Its blind spot. It looks at each timing on its own. Try B (hold 110, gap 180) also scores exactly 3.84 and gets a YES, even though nobody types like that in your tries.

  2. Mahalanobis: does the pattern fit?

    The idea. This detector also measures distance from your average, but it knows which timings move together. In your tries a longer hold always comes with a longer gap. A try that follows that pattern counts as close. A try that breaks it counts as far, even when each timing alone looks normal.

    Try A (110, 220): slower on both, like your slow tries. Score 1.83, under 2.03: YES

    Try B (110, 180): long hold but short gap, which you never do. Score 6.36, over 2.03: NO

    Both tries are the same 3.84 wobbles away for detector 1. Only this detector sees that B breaks your pattern.

  3. Nearest neighbor: does it match one of your tries?

    The idea. Instead of comparing with your average, this detector finds the single enrollment try that is closest to the new one, using the same pattern-aware distance as detector 2. If that closest try is near enough, it says yes. It helps when you sometimes type in a slightly different way, because one matching try is all it needs.

    New try C: hold 116, gap 220. Its nearest try is try 2 (112, 218), at a distance of 0.81. That is under 0.84: YES

    The same try is a little too far from the average for the other two (detector 1 gives 4.84 against 4.73, detector 2 gives 2.12 against 2.03), so they both say NO.

Where does “the line” come from?

Each detector learns its own line from your 10 tries, so nobody has to pick a number by hand. It hides one try, scores it against the other 9, and repeats that for all 10. For detector 1 that gives these 10 scores:

0.00 · 0.45 · 1.16 · 1.41 · 2.04 · 2.06 · 3.27 · 3.34 · 4.49 · 4.93

The line goes where 95% of your own scores fall below it, which here is 4.73. So roughly 1 in 20 of your real tries will still get a NO from each detector. That is why one NO is not enough to reject you.

The vote: 2 of 3 wins

Each detector gives one vote. If at least 2 of the 3 say yes, the try is accepted. Here are the example tries:

These are the same messages you see on the Try it tab after a verify.

The real thing is bigger, not different. On a keyboard the page uses all 31 timings (11 holds, 10 press-to-press times and 10 gaps). On a phone it adds 22 numbers for where your finger lands on each key, 53 in all. The three detectors and the 2-of-3 vote are exactly the same.

Where they come from. All three are classic detectors from a 2009 study by Killourhy and Maxion at Carnegie Mellon, which compared many ways of telling typists apart. Detectors 2 and 3 also soften the “moves together” pattern a little, so 10 tries are not over-trusted.

How it works

What the page does behind the scenes, from your first key press to “accepted” or “rejected”. No maths needed.

  1. Everyone types the same password

    The password is .tie5Roanl, then Return. It is printed right on the page, so it is not a secret. The page does not care much what you type. It cares how you type it.

  2. The page times every key

    For each key, the page notes the moment you press it down and the moment you let go. From that it gets two kinds of numbers:

    • Hold: how long each key stays pressed.
    • Gap: how long you wait between one key and the next.

    One full password gives 31 of these timings. On a phone, the page shows its own keypad and also notes where your finger lands on each key.

  3. You enroll by typing it 10 times

    In step 1 (Enroll) you type the password 10 times at your normal speed. A typo, Backspace or wrong key throws that try away, and you simply type it again. Only clean tries count.

  4. The page learns your rhythm

    After the 10th try, the page works out your usual hold and gap for every key, and how much they normally wobble from try to try. That set of numbers is your profile: a fingerprint made of timing instead of skin.

  5. Verify compares a new try with your profile

    In step 2 (Verify) you type the password once more. The page measures the same 31 timings and puts them next to your profile. Three separate checks each decide “close enough” or “too different”. If at least two of them say close enough, you are accepted. The Detectors tab shows how each check works, with examples.

  6. Why someone else gets rejected

    A friend can type every letter right, but they hold keys for different lengths and pause in different places. Each difference is small, but across 31 timings they add up, so the checks say “too different” and the try is rejected. To see it, enroll yourself, then hand the keyboard to someone else.

It is not perfect. Your own rhythm changes when you are tired, rushed or on a different keyboard, so sometimes it will reject you too. Treat it as a demo of the idea, used alongside a password, never instead of one.
