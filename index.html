    Detectors

    When you verify, three detectors each compare your new try with your 10 enrollment tries and answer yes or no. Here is how each one thinks, worked through with a tiny example.

    The example: your friend tries your Instagram

    Imagine your Instagram login used KeyPrint, and your password is tinycats. Your friend watched you type it at lunch and now wants to log in as you and post something embarrassing. The password alone will not stop them. Your typing rhythm has to.

    The real page measures about three timings for every key, so tinycats plus Return gives 25 per try. To keep the sums small, this example uses only two: how long you hold t, and the gap from letting go of t to pressing i. Here are your 10 enrollment tries, in milliseconds:

    Try12345678910Average
    Hold t10011288106941049610892100100
    Gap t→i200218184208192206196214186196200

    Look at try 2 and try 3. On a slow try you hold t longer and wait longer before i. On a fast try, both are shorter. Keep that in mind for detector 2.

    Then five logins come in:

    • A (hold 110, gap 220): you at 7 a.m. in bed, checking your likes before coffee. Slow, but your kind of slow.
    • B (110, 180): your friend, who knows the password, holds t like you but then rushes to i before you notice.
    • C (116, 220): you, typing one-handed on the bus with a bubble tea in the other hand.
    • D (130, 260): a scammer who bought your password from a leak, typing it slowly with one finger.
    • E (100, 205): you on a normal day, posting a story.

    1. Scaled Manhattan: how many wobbles away?

      The idea. It measures how far each timing of a new try is from your average, counted in units of your usual wobble, and adds them up. A small total means the try looks like you.

      Your usual wobble is the average distance of your 10 tries from their average: 6 ms for the hold and 9.2 ms for the gap.

      New try A (you in bed before coffee): hold 110, gap 220

      hold: (110 − 100) ÷ 6 = 1.67
      gap: (220 − 200) ÷ 9.2 = 2.17
      total = 3.84

      The line for this detector is 4.73. 3.84 is under it, so: YES

      New try D: hold 130, gap 260 (the one-finger scammer)

      hold: (130 − 100) ÷ 6 = 5.00
      gap: (260 − 200) ÷ 9.2 = 6.52
      total = 11.52

      11.52 is over 4.73, so: NO

      Its blind spot. It looks at each timing on its own. Try B (hold 110, gap 180) also scores exactly 3.84 and gets a YES. That is your friend, and detector 1 just let them into your account.

    2. Mahalanobis: does the pattern fit?

      The idea. It also measures distance from your average, but it knows which timings move together (for you, a longer hold comes with a longer gap). A try that breaks that pattern counts as far, even when each timing alone looks normal.

      Try A (110, 220): you before coffee, slower on both like your slow tries. Score 1.83, under 2.03: YES

      Try B (110, 180): your friend: long hold but short gap, which you never do. Score 6.36, over 2.03: NO

      Both tries are the same 3.84 wobbles away for detector 1. Only this detector sees that B breaks your pattern.

    3. Nearest neighbor: does it match one of your tries?

      The idea. Instead of your average, it finds the one enrollment try closest to the new try (using detector 2’s pattern-aware distance) and says yes if it is near enough. That helps when you sometimes type differently, because one matching try is all it needs.

      New try C (you on the bus): hold 116, gap 220. Its nearest try is try 2 (112, 218), at a distance of 0.81. That is under 0.84: YES

      The same try is a little too far from the average for the other two (detector 1 gives 4.84 against 4.73, detector 2 gives 2.12 against 2.03), so they both say NO.

      Sadly 1 vote is not enough (see the vote below), so you have to put the bubble tea down and type again.

    Where does “the line” come from?

    Each detector learns its own line from your 10 tries, so nobody has to pick a number by hand. It hides one try, scores it against the other 9, and repeats that for all 10. For detector 1 that gives these 10 scores:

    0.00 · 0.45 · 1.16 · 1.41 · 2.04 · 2.06 · 3.27 · 3.34 · 4.49 · 4.93

    The line goes where 95% of your own scores fall below it, which here is 4.73. So roughly 1 in 20 of your real tries will still get a NO from each detector. That is why one NO is not enough to reject you.

    The vote: 2 of 3 wins

    Each detector gives one vote. If at least 2 of the 3 say yes, the try is accepted. So, who gets into your account?

    Try (hold, gap)1 · Manhattan2 · Mahalanobis3 · NearestResult
    A (110, 220)YESYESYESAccepted: 3 of 3 detectors say it is you.
    E (100, 205)YESYESNOAccepted: 2 of 3 detectors say it is you.
    B (110, 180)YESNONORejected: only 1 of 3 detectors say it is you.
    C (116, 220)NONOYESRejected: only 1 of 3 detectors say it is you.
    D (130, 260)NONONORejected: only 0 of 3 detectors say it is you.

    Only you before coffee (A) and you on a normal day (E) get in. Your friend (B) and the scammer (D) are locked out, and you on the bus (C) just have to put the bubble tea down and type again.

    The Try it tab keeps it short: an accepted try just says It is you., and a rejected one gets a cheeky line instead of the vote count.

    5 real-world ways this helps

    1. Leaked passwords. Huge password leaks happen all the time, and many people reuse the same password everywhere. Whoever buys your leaked password has the right letters but not your rhythm, so they get a NO.
    2. Brute-force and bot attacks. Hackers use scripts that fire thousands of password guesses. Even when a script hits the right password, it still has to match your hold and gap timings, which it has never seen, so it gets a NO. Pasting the password is blocked here too.
    3. Someone saw you type it. A friend, a coworker or a shoulder-surfer in a café can learn your password by watching. Knowing the password is not enough; they also have to type it the way you do, like your friend in the Instagram story.
    4. A second check without an extra device. SMS codes and security keys need a phone or gadget you can lose. Your rhythm is always with you and checks you in the background while you type the password you already know.
    5. Proving the same person is still there. Some banks watch typing behavior to spot a stranger on a logged-in account, and online courses have used typing rhythm to check that the same student does the work.

    How it works

    What the page does behind the scenes, from your first key press to “accepted” or “rejected”. No maths needed.

    1. You make your own password

      First you create a password: at least 8 characters, with a capital letter, a number and a special character such as ! or #. You also write your own security question and its answer. The page keeps only scrambled versions (salted hashes) of the password and the answer, so the words themselves are never stored. Getting the password right is only half of it: the page also cares how you type it.

    2. The page times every key

      For each key, the page notes the moment you press it down and the moment you let go. From that it gets two kinds of numbers:

      • Hold: how long each key stays pressed.
      • Gap: how long you wait between one key and the next.

      Every key gives about three timings, so an 8-character password plus Return gives 25 of them, and a longer password gives more. On a phone, the page shows its own keyboard and also notes where your finger lands on each key.

    3. You enroll by typing it 10 times

      In step 1 (Enroll) you type your password 10 times at your normal speed. Backspace throws that try away, and a wrong password is not counted, so you simply type it again. Only clean, correct tries count.

    4. The page learns your rhythm

      After the 10th try, the page works out your usual hold and gap for every key, and how much they normally wobble from try to try. That set of numbers is your profile: a fingerprint made of timing instead of skin.

    5. Verify compares a new try with your profile

      In step 2 (Verify) you type the password once more. If it is the wrong password, that is the end of it: rejected. If it is right, the page measures the same timings and puts them next to your profile. Three separate checks each decide “close enough” or “too different”. If at least two of them say close enough, you are accepted. The Detectors tab shows how each check works, with examples.

    6. Why someone else gets rejected

      Tell a friend your password. They can type every letter right, but they hold keys for different lengths and pause in different places. Each difference is small, but across all those timings they add up, so the checks say “too different” and the try is rejected. To see it, enroll yourself, then hand the keyboard to someone else.

    7. Rejected but it really is you? Answer your question

      Your own rhythm can drift. Under the password box on the Verify step there is an Is it really you? link, before you type and after any rejection. Tap it, answer your security question correctly, and you are let in, and the page offers to retrain on how you type now. Reset on the Enroll step wipes everything so you can start over with a new password.

    It is not perfect. Your own rhythm changes when you are tired, rushed or on a different keyboard, so sometimes it will reject you too. Treat it as a demo of the idea, used alongside a password, never instead of one.
